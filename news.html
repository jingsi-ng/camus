The Plague Continues to Resonate in Post-Pandemic World

Five years after the COVID-19 pandemic began, Albert Camus's 1947 novel The Plague continues to find new readers seeking meaning in times of crisis. The novel, which depicts a fictional plague outbreak in the Algerian city of Oran, has seen sustained interest since 2020.

"Camus understood something fundamental about how societies respond to catastrophe," says Dr. Sarah Mitchell, professor of French Literature at University College Dublin. "His exploration of solidarity, denial, and the search for meaning in suffering speaks directly to our recent collective experience."

Publishers report that sales of The Plague increased by over 1,000% during the early months of the pandemic and have remained significantly higher than pre-2020 levels [10]. The novel has been translated into over 30 languages and continues to appear on bestseller lists worldwide.

"The only way to fight the plague is with decency." — Dr. Rieux, The Plague

Scholars note that Camus's emphasis on collective action and individual responsibility offers a philosophical framework for understanding public health responses and the moral obligations of citizenship during times of crisis.

New Collection of Camus Letters Reveals Personal Side of the Philosopher

A previously unpublished collection of Albert Camus's personal correspondence has been released by French publisher Gallimard, offering new insights into the private life of one of the 20th century's most influential writers.

The collection, titled Correspondance 1944-1959, includes over 200 letters written to friends, family members, and fellow intellectuals during the most productive period of Camus's career. The letters reveal a more vulnerable and humorous side of the author than is typically seen in his published works.

"These letters show Camus as a warm, passionate man who struggled with fame and deeply missed his Algerian homeland," notes editor Marie-Thérèse Blondeau. "They add important context to understanding both his literary works and his philosophical positions."

Of particular interest are exchanges with Jean-Paul Sartre before their famous falling-out, as well as previously unknown letters to his mother, who could not read [2]. These letters were often read to her by family members and reveal Camus's deep attachment to his humble origins.

"Everything I know about morality and the obligations of men, I owe to football." — Albert Camus, letter to a friend, 1957

The collection also includes Camus's reflections on winning the Nobel Prize, which he described as arriving "too soon" and bringing with it a burden of expectations that made writing more difficult. An English translation is expected in late 2025.
