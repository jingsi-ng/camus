Albert Camus Society Celebrates 20th Anniversary with Dublin Conference

The Albert Camus Society will hold its 2025 annual conference at Technological University Dublin (TU Dublin), marking the organisation's 20th anniversary. The conference brings together international scholars to discuss Camus's continuing relevance in philosophy, literature, and contemporary thought [14].

Founded in 2005, the Albert Camus Society is an international organisation comprising the UK, US, and Polish Camus societies. Together they publish the Journal of Camus Studies and host annual conferences exploring Camus's work from philosophical, theological, and literary perspectives [15].

This year's programme features presentations on topics including Camus's early writings on the sacred, his critique of Hegel, the role of empathy in The Fall, and reassessments of his complex relationship with colonial Algeria. Scholars continue to debate whether Camus should be viewed as a "secular saint" or a "good-willed colonizer"—a tension that remains central to contemporary Camus scholarship.

"The Albert Camus Society exists to promote scholarship on the life and work of Albert Camus by providing an international platform to share, exchange and debate ideas." — Albert Camus Society Mission Statement

The choice of Dublin as the venue reflects the Society's international reach and the growing interest in Camus studies across Europe. Previous conferences have been held in London, Kraków, and virtually during the pandemic years.

Camus's The Plague Becomes Bestseller as COVID-19 Spreads

As the COVID-19 pandemic swept across the world in early 2020, readers turned to Albert Camus's 1947 novel The Plague in unprecedented numbers. The British publisher Penguin Classics reported struggling to keep up with demand, with sales jumping from hundreds to thousands per month [16].

According to New Statesman, UK sales of the English translation increased by more than 1,000 percent. In Japan, more copies sold in March 2020 than in the previous 31 years combined, with at least one bookshop rationing copies to one per customer [17].

Sales in Italy tripled, placing the novel in the country's top 10 bestsellers during the nationwide lockdown. In France, sales peaked at more than 1,600 copies in a single week—an increase of around 300 percent compared to the previous year [16].

"The message of La Peste rings true today as it did back then, as it will in the future. I'm glad to learn people are reading it again." — Catherine Camus, Albert Camus's daughter, to The Guardian

Camus's daughter Catherine, who has managed her father's literary legacy for 40 years, told The Guardian that she hoped readers would find hope in the novel's pages. "We are not responsible for coronavirus but we can be responsible in the way we respond to it," she said. "Papa tried to speak for those who had no words of their own." [16]
