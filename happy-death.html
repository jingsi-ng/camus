A Happy Death

La Mort heureuse

Written: 1936-1938 Published: 1971 (posthumously) Genre: Novel

Camus's first novel, written in his twenties and published after his death, follows Patrice Mersault as he seeks happiness through freedom. The novel served as a testing ground for themes later perfected in The Stranger[12].

Overview

A Happy Death tells the story of Patrice Mersault (note the similar name to Meursault in The Stranger), a young Algerian clerk who kills a wealthy invalid named Zagreus and takes his money. Unlike The Stranger, this murder is premeditated—Mersault sees it as a means to achieve the time and freedom necessary for happiness [12].

The novel is divided into two parts: "Natural Death" and "Conscious Death." In the first part, Mersault commits his crime. In the second, he uses his newfound wealth to travel, love, and ultimately find a way to die happy [13].

The Question of Happiness

Unlike The Stranger, which focuses on absurdity and alienation, A Happy Death is explicitly about the pursuit of happiness. Camus explores whether happiness is possible in an absurd world, and what conditions might allow for it: time, freedom, connection with nature, and conscious living.

"It is not true that the heart wears out—but the body creates this illusion." — Albert Camus, A Happy Death

Autobiographical Elements

The novel is heavily autobiographical. Like Mersault, young Camus was poor, lived in Algeria, and was diagnosed with tuberculosis. The Mediterranean landscapes, the poverty of Algiers, and the yearning for a life fully lived all reflect Camus's own experience [1].

Camus chose not to publish the novel during his lifetime, considering it too rough and personal. It was published in 1971, eleven years after his death, and offers valuable insight into the development of his thought [12].

Connection to The Stranger

A Happy Death can be read as a first draft of The Stranger. Both feature protagonists named Mersault/Meursault, both involve murder, and both explore themes of alienation and authenticity. However, while The Stranger focuses on the absurd, A Happy Death is more optimistic, suggesting that happiness—though difficult—is achievable.

Key Themes

Happiness and Freedom

Mersault believes that happiness requires freedom, and freedom requires money—or at least freedom from the need to work. His crime is motivated by the desire to escape poverty and live fully [12].

Conscious Death

The title refers to dying with full awareness and acceptance—not in despair, but in the knowledge that one has lived fully. Mersault achieves this by the novel's end [13].

The Mediterranean

Sun, sea, and physical sensation are central to Camus's vision of happiness. The Mediterranean represents life, warmth, and sensory pleasure—a counterpoint to abstract philosophy [1].
