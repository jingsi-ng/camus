Novels
  • 1942

    The Stranger

    L'Étranger

    Novel

    Mother died today. Or maybe yesterday; I can't be sure.

    "I summarized The Stranger a long time ago, with a remark I admit was highly paradoxical: 'In our society any man who does not weep at his mother's funeral runs the risk of being sentenced to death.' I only meant that the hero of my book is condemned because he does not play the game. He refuses to lie."[1]

    Read More →
  • 1947

    The Plague

    La Peste

    Novel

    The evil that is in the world always comes of ignorance, and good intentions may do as much harm as malevolence, if they lack understanding.

    "I want to express by means of the plague the stifling air from which we all suffered and the atmosphere of threat and exile in which we lived. I want at the same time to extend that interpretation to the notion of existence in general."[2]

  • 1956

    The Fall

    La Chute

    Novel

    I'll tell you a great secret, my friend. Don't wait for the last judgment. It happens every day.

    The Fall is a confession. Many readers felt exposed by it—they saw themselves in Jean-Baptiste Clamence, a man who discovered that his good deeds were done only for the pleasure of self-admiration.[3]

  • 1971

    A Happy Death

    La Mort Heureuse

    Novel

    Should I kill myself, or have a cup of coffee?

    Camus wrote this novel between 1936-1938 but chose not to publish it during his lifetime. He considered it a "workshop" for The Stranger, exploring similar themes of authenticity and death. The manuscript was found among his papers and published eleven years after his death.[4]

    Read More →
  • 1994

    The First Man

    Le Premier Homme

    Novel

    He was born in a country that was not his own, and that would become so.

    This unfinished autobiographical novel was found in the wreckage of the car accident that killed Camus in 1960. The manuscript, splattered with mud, was in his briefcase. His daughter Catherine published it 34 years later.[5]

  • 1957

    Exile and the Kingdom

    L'Exil et le Royaume

    Stories

    To grow old is to pass from passion to compassion.

    These six short stories explore the theme of exile—physical, spiritual, and emotional. Camus wrote them during a period of personal crisis in the mid-1950s when he felt increasingly isolated from both the Left and the Right in French intellectual life.[6]

Philosophical Essays
  • 1942

    The Myth of Sisyphus

    Le Mythe de Sisyphe

    Essay

    One must imagine Sisyphus happy.

    "The fundamental subject of The Myth of Sisyphus is this: it is legitimate and necessary to wonder whether life has a meaning; therefore it is legitimate to meet the problem of suicide face to face. The answer is this: even if one does not believe in God, suicide is not legitimate."[7]

    Read More →
  • 1951

    The Rebel

    L'Homme Révolté

    Essay

    I rebel — therefore we exist.

    This philosophical treatise caused Camus's famous break with Sartre. Camus argued that revolution inevitably leads to tyranny when it loses its foundation in human solidarity. Sartre's journal Les Temps Modernes published a devastating critique, ending their friendship.[8]

  • 1939

    Nuptials

    Noces

    Essays

    In the midst of winter, I found there was, within me, an invincible summer.

    These lyrical essays celebrate the sensual beauty of Algeria and the Mediterranean. Written in his mid-twenties, they express Camus's deep love for his homeland—the sun, the sea, the ancient ruins of Tipasa.[6]

  • 1954

    Summer

    L'Été

    Essays

    There is merely bad luck in not being loved; there is misfortune in not loving.

    A collection of essays written between 1939 and 1953, returning to the Mediterranean themes of Nuptials. Includes "Return to Tipasa," where Camus famously rediscovered the "invincible summer" within himself.[9]

  • 1961

    Resistance, Rebellion, and Death

    Résistance, Révolte et Mort

    Essays

    Freedom is nothing but a chance to be better.

    This posthumous collection gathers Camus's political essays and journalism, including his writings from Combat during the Resistance, his opposition to capital punishment, and his tortured reflections on the Algerian War.[10]

  • 1945

    Letters to a German Friend

    Lettres à un ami allemand

    Essays

    I should like to be able to love my country and still love justice.

    Four open letters written during the Nazi Occupation, addressed to an imaginary German friend. Camus articulates his moral opposition to Nazism while refusing the easy comfort of patriotic hatred.[11]

Plays
  • 1944

    Caligula

    Caligula

    Play

    Men die and they are not happy.

    "Caligula, a seemingly kind prince, realises upon the death of Drusilla that men die and they are not happy. Obsessed by the quest for the Absolute, a freedom which he discovers in the end is not truly freedom. Caligula is the story of a superior suicide."[12]

  • 1944

    The Misunderstanding

    Le Malentendu

    Play

    A man is more a man through the things he keeps to himself than through those he says.

    A dark tragedy inspired by a newspaper clipping about a son who returns home unrecognized and is murdered by his mother and sister. Camus saw in this the impossibility of human communication and the cruelty of a silent universe.[13]

  • 1949

    The Just Assassins

    Les Justes

    Play

    Real generosity toward the future lies in giving all to the present.

    Based on true events: the 1905 assassination of Grand Duke Sergei of Russia. Camus was fascinated by revolutionaries who set moral limits on their violence—Kaliayev refused to throw his bomb when he saw children in the carriage.[12]

  • 1948

    State of Siege

    L'État de Siège

    Play

    I have decided to assume the task of making you happy by force.

    An allegorical play where the Plague arrives as a character in the Spanish city of Cadiz, establishing a totalitarian regime. It was Camus's most experimental play.[12]

  • 1959

    The Possessed

    Les Possédés

    Play

    If there is no God, then everything is permitted.

    Camus adapted Dostoevsky's novel The Possessed (Demons) for the stage, a project that occupied him for twenty years. He saw in Dostoevsky's nihilists a prophetic warning about revolutionary ideology untethered from human values.[14]

Early Works & Notebooks
  • 1937

    The Wrong Side and the Right Side

    L'Envers et l'Endroit

    Essays

    There is no love of life without despair of life.

    "Whatever I may write hereafter, I know that the secret of my craft is to be found in this first book. Even now, after twenty years, I cannot re-read certain pages without being overwhelmed by a kind of dizziness."[9]

  • 1962

    Notebooks

    Carnets

    Notebooks

    Live to the point of tears.

    Camus kept detailed notebooks from 1935 until his death. They contain early drafts of his works, philosophical reflections, and personal confessions. He called them "neither a diary nor a work—a means of keeping in hand my thoughts and my pen."[15]
