The Myth of Sisyphus

Le Mythe de Sisyphe

Published: 1942 Genre: Philosophical Essay Pages: 212

Camus's foundational philosophical essay explores the concept of the absurd and asks the most fundamental question of philosophy: is life worth living? The answer, surprisingly, is a resounding yes[8].

Overview

The Myth of Sisyphus opens with one of the most famous lines in philosophy: "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide."[8] Camus argues that we must confront the fundamental absurdity of existence before we can begin to live authentically.

The absurd, for Camus, arises from the conflict between our human desire for meaning and the universe's silent indifference. We want answers, but the world offers none. This confrontation between human need and cosmic silence is what Camus calls "the absurd" [3].

The Absurd Hero

Camus uses the Greek myth of Sisyphus as his central metaphor. Sisyphus was condemned by the gods to roll a boulder up a hill for eternity, only to watch it roll back down each time. This, Camus argues, is the perfect image of the human condition: endless, meaningless labor [11].

"The absurd is born of the confrontion between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world." Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus

Philosophical Suicide

Camus rejects both physical suicide and what he calls "philosophical suicide" - the leap of faith into religion or abstract systems that promise meaning. Instead, he advocates for living fully in the face of absurdity, embracing life's experiences without the crutch of false hope [3].

The Absurd Creator

Camus concludes by discussing the absurd creator—the artist who creates without hope of eternal recognition, who works for the work itself. Art, for Camus, is not an escape from the absurd but a way of living within it, multiplying experiences and perspectives [11].

Key Themes

The Absurd

The confrontation between human reason's demand for meaning and the universe's unreasonable silence. This gap is not to be resolved but lived within [3].

Revolt

The proper response to absurdity is not despair but revolt—a constant confrontation with the absurd, refusing to accept false consolations while affirming life [8].

Living Without Appeal

True freedom comes from accepting that there is no higher meaning to appeal to—no god, no eternal values. This acceptance liberates us to create our own meaning through action [11].
