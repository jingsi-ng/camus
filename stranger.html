The Stranger

L'Étranger

Published: 1942 Genre: Novel Pages: 123

Camus's first novel tells the story of Meursault, a French Algerian who kills an Arab man and is tried and sentenced to death. The novel explores themes of absurdism, alienation, and the indifference of the universe[4].

Overview

The Stranger opens with one of the most famous lines in literature: "Mother died today. Or maybe yesterday; I can't be sure."[4] This opening immediately establishes Meursault's emotional detachment and sets the tone for the entire novel.

The protagonist, Meursault, is a young French Algerian clerk who attends his mother's funeral with a disturbing lack of emotion. He then begins a casual relationship with Marie, helps his neighbor Raymond with a personal matter, and eventually commits a senseless murder on a beach [9].

The Murder and Trial

The murder itself is described in strangely detached terms. Meursault shoots an Arab man, claiming the sun was in his eyes. During his trial, the prosecution focuses less on the murder itself and more on Meursault's behavior at his mother's funeral—his lack of tears, his cigarette smoking, his beginning a romantic affair the very next day [4].

"I opened myself to the gentle indifference of the world." Meursault, The Stranger

Philosophical Significance

The Stranger is Camus's literary exploration of absurdism. Meursault is condemned not for his crime, but for his refusal to play society's emotional games. He won't pretend to feel what he doesn't feel, won't lie to make others comfortable. In this sense, he represents the absurd hero—someone who lives authentically in an indifferent universe [3].

The novel was published in the same year as The Myth of Sisyphus and the two works complement each other: the essay provides the philosophy, while the novel provides its human embodiment [1].

Key Themes

The Absurd

Meursault embodies the absurd man—someone aware of life's meaninglessness yet continuing to live. His indifference isn't apathy; it's honesty about the human condition [3].

Social Conformity

Society condemns Meursault not for murder but for failing to conform to expected emotional responses. His refusal to cry at his mother's funeral becomes evidence of his "inhumanity" [4].

Physical World

Meursault is intensely attuned to physical sensations—the sun, the sea, Marie's body. This focus on the physical over the emotional or spiritual is central to his character and Camus's philosophy [9].
